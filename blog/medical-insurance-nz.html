Medical Insurance NZ: What It Covers and How to Decide

A plain-English guide to private health cover, the public system, exclusions, excesses and the questions worth comparing.

Medical insurance in New Zealand can help pay for eligible private specialist consultations, diagnostic tests, surgery and other treatment, subject to the policy's limits, exclusions and underwriting. It does not replace the public health system. The practical question is whether the extra access and certainty offered by a particular policy are worth its ongoing cost for you.

How medical insurance fits alongside public healthcare

New Zealand citizens and residence-class visa holders are generally eligible for publicly funded health services. Eligibility does not mean every service is automatically available: clinical need, prioritisation and funding determine which services a person receives.

Waiting time also varies by treatment and region. The Ministry of Health reported that, at the end of the 2024/25 financial year, 63.9% of patients who had been accepted for elective treatment had waited less than four months. MidCentral was higher at 81.4%. The national target is for 95% to wait less than four months by 2030. Those figures describe the public system as a whole; they cannot predict one person's treatment date.

Private medical insurance creates another possible pathway for treatment covered by the policy. It may give you more control over timing, specialist or facility, but none of those outcomes should be assumed without checking the policy and the provider's availability.

What private health cover may pay for

Policies differ materially. Depending on the plan, cover may include:

  • specialist consultations and diagnostic imaging;
  • eligible surgical and non-surgical treatment in a private facility;
  • cancer treatment or medicines, within stated benefits and definitions;
  • optional day-to-day benefits such as GP, dental, optical or physiotherapy costs.

The Financial Markets Authority recommends looking at what a policy does and does not cover, not price alone. A lower premium can come with a higher excess, narrower benefits, lower limits or more exclusions.

The policy details that matter most

Pre-existing conditions and underwriting

An insurer may exclude a condition, apply a special term, charge a different premium or decline cover after assessing the application. The outcome depends on the applicant, the condition and the insurer's underwriting. Disclose health information fully and check the written decision rather than assuming a condition is covered.

Excess, benefit limits and eligible treatment

The excess is the amount you contribute to an eligible claim. A higher excess can reduce the premium, but it also increases what you pay when treatment is needed. Check annual or lifetime limits, whether diagnostic tests require prior approval, and which providers or facilities are recognised.

Premium structure and future affordability

The starting premium is only one part of the decision. Ask how premiums can change with age, medical-cost inflation, claims experience or insurer repricing. Consider whether the cover would still be affordable later, and what options exist to change the excess or benefits without replacing the whole policy.

Stand-down periods and exclusions

Some benefits may not be available immediately after a policy starts. The duration and scope vary, so use the policy wording and schedule for the exact terms. Also check exclusions for treatment outside New Zealand, non-approved providers, cosmetic treatment and experimental treatment.

Five questions to ask before choosing a policy

  1. Which treatments and diagnostics are covered, and which are excluded?
  2. What excess would I pay, and does it apply per claim, per condition or per policy year?
  3. How were my disclosed health conditions treated in the underwriting decision?
  4. What benefit limits, prior-approval rules and recognised-provider rules apply?
  5. How could the premium change, and what can I adjust if affordability becomes difficult?

So, is medical insurance worth it?

There is no universal answer. It may be more valuable if access to eligible private treatment, control over timing or avoiding a large unexpected private-treatment bill matters to you. It may be less urgent if the public pathway meets your needs, you could comfortably self-fund likely costs, or the premium would displace more important household priorities.

Compare the actual policy terms against your budget and priorities. If you are considering replacing existing cover, check the underwriting consequences first: a new policy can treat health conditions differently from the policy you already hold.
